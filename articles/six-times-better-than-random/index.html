Which model picks the right job postings?

I tested free, local models on 500 job postings they had never seen, to find which one should decide when I get a job alert. The best result came from chaining simple rules and two models. About two in five of its alerts were good jobs, six times better than picking at random.

  2. 2One yes/no questionA decision model (Nimble 9B) answers "Is this a software role?"

The test

500 postings, each labelled in advance

I picked 500 postings at random from four days of my job search. A larger LLM read each one with my full resume and marked 35 as good jobs, meaning postings I'd want an alert for. I didn't check those labels by hand, so the scores show how often each approach agreed with it.

Every check includes my resume, so I kept everything on my own machine. I also wanted it free, which rules out paid hosted models such as Jev.

Scoring

Two ways to score an alert system

Every approach was judged on two separate questions:

  • Alerts worth opening: of the alerts it sent, how many were good jobs?
  • Good jobs caught: of the 35 good jobs, how many did it alert me to?

Low on the first means noisy alerts; low on the second means missed jobs. For example, an approach that sends 10 alerts, 4 of them good jobs, has 40% of its alerts worth opening, and it caught 4 of the 35 good jobs.

You can't max out both. Alert on every posting and you catch all 35 good jobs, but only 7% of the alerts are worth opening. Alert on almost nothing and most alerts are good, but most good jobs are missed. So the leaderboard ranks by alerts worth opening, because those are the alerts I read every day, and shows good jobs caught and the number of alerts beside it, so you can see what each approach gives up.

Reading the leaderboard

What this data means

Reading the top row. The hybrid pipeline sent 43 alerts across the 500 postings. 18 of them were good jobs, so 42% were worth opening, and those 18 are about half of the 35 good jobs.

Why not a row that catches more? The next row, rules + classifier, catches 26 instead of 18 but sends more than twice as many alerts, and about three in four of them are noise.

The LLM prompt caught all 6 good jobs in its smaller sample, but only by alerting on almost half of all postings. Four in five of its alerts weren't worth opening.

What I learned

Three takeaways

  • Filter with rules before asking a model.Plain checks on location, experience and job title more than doubled the share of the classifier's alerts worth opening, and they cost nothing.
  • Ask a decision model one specific question."Is this a software role?" lost no good jobs. Asked "Should I get an alert?", only one alert in six was worth opening.
  • Check a model on your own task before trusting its benchmark.GLiNER2.5-Decide, a small open decision model, beats my classifier on its maker's own benchmark, yet it said "alert" to 499 of the 500 postings.

The LLM prompt (gemma4:e4b through Ollama, thinking on) ran on a random 60 of the 500 postings. Times are on a 24 GB M3 MacBook.
