Job alerts

Can a 400M decision model replace a local 4B LLM for job alerts?

Decision models pick an answer from a fixed list and give a probability for each one, instead of writing text. I tested several against the local LLM my job-alert pipeline started with. A 400-million-parameter decision model, asked three narrow questions about each posting, ranks jobs about as well as the LLM, makes the pipeline about 60 times faster and explains every skip. The bigger lesson was not the model: cheap rules in front decided most of the result, and made a separate role-check model unnecessary.

The setup

Twelve job boards, one question per posting

My job search pulls postings from 12 boards and decides, for each one, whether it deserves an email alert, a save for later, or nothing. A local LLM writing a 1–10 match score worked, but slowly, and its reasons were free text. Each check has a fixed question and fixed answers, which is what decision models are made for. Two rules for trying them: everything runs on my own machine, because every check includes my resume, and it costs nothing.

General LLM · 4B · local

Gemma (gemma4:e4b)

Decision model · 400M · fine-tuned

ModernBERT-based (convaiinnovations/laya), fine-tuned on my labelled jobs. Asked one question, then three.

From Bespoke Labs and Cloudflare, through Ollama. Used for one question: "Is this a software engineering role?"

Three narrow questions

Split the fuzzy judgement

My first Laya answered one question, "Should the candidate be considered?" (notify, log or skip), which mixes three judgements. The version below asks them separately and in order; a posting that fails a step is skipped, and the grey boxes are the log lines that say why.

1

Which stack is this role?

Backend, frontend, full stack, AI, data, infrastructure or other, from the posting alone. Backend, full stack, AI and data go on.

skip · skipped, stack role frontend
2

Is there a dealbreaker?

Clearance, citizenship, a senior level, the wrong kind of role. 50% or more skips.

skip · skipped, likely dealbreaker (72%)
3

How well does it fit?

A 1–10 score from my resume and the posting: the probability-weighted average of each score.

alert · fit 7.02/10, at or above the cut-off

A 1–10 score trains better than a yes/no "good fit?": a yes/no Laya reached only 24% of alerts worth opening, and the score gives the yes/no answer for free (the chance of a 7 or more).

The test

500 unseen postings, judged two ways

I took 500 postings at random from four days of my search. A larger LLM read each with my full resume and marked 35 as good jobs, 7%. Every setup is scored on alerts worth opening (the share of its alerts that were good) and good jobs caught (of the 35); you can't max out both. The ground truth is an LLM, not my own judgement, and Laya was trained on its labels while Gemma never saw them, so Laya starts with an advantage.

Round 1

Each model alone: none is good enough

Asked the whole question, every model sends far too many alerts. A newer decision model does no better: strands-decider sends 284 alerts, 12.3% worth opening. tev1 4B could not take the question at all: it accepts at most 2,048 tokens, and a resume plus a posting is longer for 113 of the 500. GLiNER2.5-Decide leads its maker's benchmark, yet it said "alert" to 499 of 500. What helped was putting cheap rules in front.

It is a trade-off, not a ranking. When both catch the same number of good jobs, their alerts are about as clean: catching 16, 40% of Laya's alerts are worth opening against 36% of Gemma's; catching 32, 30% against 31%. Laya's own cut-off stops at 15 good jobs, short of the 18 I aimed for, while Gemma at 8 catches 32 with three times the alerts. Over all cut-offs, ranking quality (average precision) is 0.40 for Laya and 0.32 for Gemma, and the interval of the difference includes zero.

Under today's rules. When the model went live I tightened the rules to skip senior titles and contracts. Replayed with them, the benchmark keeps 20 good jobs instead of 34 (10 senior and 4 contract roles that the labels count as good), and Laya's 21 alerts catch 7 of the 20 good jobs left, and 33% of them are worth opening.

The front of the pipeline

With rules in place, the role check adds almost nothing

Replacing Laya's stack-role and dealbreaker answers with perfect ones changed the alerts by at most one posting: the rules had already removed those jobs. Dropping Nimble and letting Laya's own checks do its job gave exactly the same result, 35 alerts with 15 good, so my live search now runs without it. Speed

About 60× faster, once the role-check model was gone

How good can it get

The labeller disagrees with itself near the line

I had the labelling LLM rate 120 benchmark postings again. It kept 34 of the 35 good jobs, but called only 10 of 30 borderline ones good the second time. Weighted back to the benchmark, a second pass reaches about 70% of alerts worth opening (somewhere between 50% and 78%) while catching 97% of good jobs. The labels themselves are fuzzy at the borderline, which caps any model trained on them.

Limits

What this benchmark can't tell you

  • It was reused. The same 500 postings judged five Laya retrains, so the best of them is likely a little optimistic.
  • The ground truth is an LLM that Laya was trained to imitate; alert emails now carry feedback buttons to measure it on my own judgement.
  • Small numbers. Thirty-five good jobs make every interval wide.
  • A few near-copies. 5 benchmark postings have near-identical copies in the training data under other ids; none of them is alerted.
What I learned

Six things about decision models in practice

  • Ask one specific question.Asked "Is this a software role?", Nimble lost no good jobs; asked "Should I get an alert?", one alert in six was worth opening.
  • Order beats model choice.Every model did far better behind cheap rules, and once the rules were there, swapping or removing the role-check model changed nothing.
  • Test on your own task.A model that leads its maker's benchmark said "alert" to 499 of 500 postings here.
  • Split a fuzzy judgement into narrow questions.A small model on three questions tied the LLM and explains every skip.
  • Compare curves, and time the whole pipeline.At one cut-off Laya looked better than the LLM; over all of them it ties. With Nimble still in front, a 60× faster scorer made only an 8× faster pipeline; timing the whole thing is what showed Nimble had to go.
  • Re-measure when the rules change.Tightening one filter dropped 14 of the 34 good jobs the benchmark kept, and the headline with it.
