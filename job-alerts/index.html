Splitting one fuzzy question into three clear ones

My job search uses a small local model, Laya, to decide which postings deserve an alert. Earlier retrains asked it one broad question, "should this job alert?", and the best reached 24% of alerts worth opening. Rebuilding it around three narrow questions brought a retrained model up to the level of my best one, made every decision explainable, and showed where alert quality actually comes from.

The starting point

Good labels, one overloaded question

Before changing the model I checked the training labels. The labelling LLM rated 120 benchmark postings a second time and agreed with its first answers on about 95% of them, keeping 34 of the 35 good jobs good. Treated as a model, that second pass gets about 70% of alerts worth opening: the realistic ceiling for this setup.

So the labels were sound. The problem was the question. "Should this alert?" mixes three judgements at once: is it my kind of role, does anything rule me out, and how well do my skills match. A 400-million-parameter model has to untangle all three from one yes/no answer.

How it decides

Three questions, asked in order, each with only the text it needs

Laya answers multiple-choice, yes/no and 1–10 questions, and its documentation shows a version trained on several question types at once. Each question below reads only what it needs. A posting and my resume together fit in 1,024 tokens for none of the 500 benchmark postings, so the first two questions read the posting alone, and only the fit question reads the resume as well.

The reasons on the right are the formats the live search writes; the numbers in them are examples.

Which stack is this role?

Seven choices: backend, frontend, full stack, AI, data, infrastructure, other. Reads the posting, up to 1,024 tokens.

How well does it fit?

The same 1–10 match score the labelling LLM gives, read from resume and posting, up to 2,048 tokens. Laya returns a probability for each level, so the score has decimals.

alert · fit 6.52/10, at or above the alert cut-offsave · fit 5.63/10, below the alert cut-offskip · fit 4.12/10, below the save cut-off

I had about 3,300 labelled postings; about 2,700 trained the model and the rest were held out for checking. Copies and near-copies of benchmark postings were removed first, so the benchmark stayed unseen. Training took four rounds of about 5 minutes on a cloud GPU.

Choosing the alert cut-off

The fit score is a dial between fewer alerts and more good jobs caught

The benchmark is the same 500 labelled postings as the earlier pages, with 35 good jobs. Rules and a yes/no software-role check (Nimble) run first, and Laya judges the 156 postings that get through. Each fit cut-off gives a different trade: a high one sends few, mostly good alerts; a low one catches more good jobs among more alerts.

Where alert quality comes from

A perfect dealbreaker check would change nothing

The dealbreaker question looked like the obvious next thing to improve. Before spending another training run, I tested its best case: I replaced the model's stack-role and dealbreaker answers with the labels' own answers, a perfect version of both, and scored the benchmark again.

Checks before the fit scoreFit 5.82 or higherFit 6.07 or higher
As trained38.6% · 22 caught41.7% · 15 caught
No dealbreaker check at all39.7% · 23 caught41.7% · 15 caught
Perfect dealbreaker39.7% · 23 caught41.7% · 15 caught
Perfect dealbreaker and stack role39.7% · 23 caught41.7% · 15 caught

The rules and the software-role check already remove nearly every dealbreaker and wrong-stack posting before Laya sees one. What decides the alerts is how well the fit score orders the postings that are left. That points the next effort at the fit score, and away from the filters.

The 41.7% here and the 42.9% above differ by one posting that sits exactly on the cut-off.

No posting was skipped for a dealbreaker: the rules had already removed them. About one posting in a hundred becomes an alert. Each of those alerts now carries feedback buttons, so whether they were worth opening gets recorded from real use, beyond the 500-posting benchmark.

What I learned

Four lessons for a small job-matching model

  • Check the labels before the model. A second labelling pass set a realistic ceiling (about 70% worth opening) to aim at.
  • Narrow questions beat one broad one. The same small model went from 24% to 42.9% of alerts worth opening, and its answers became easy to read and debug.
  • Test the best case before building it. Swapping in perfect answers showed a better dealbreaker check could not help, saving a training run.
  • The fit score is where the gap is. Closing the distance to 70% likely means a larger model reranking the few postings that pass the rules each run, with the feedback buttons supplying fresh labels.
Everything runs locally and free on my own machine; training used a cloud GPU for about 20 minutes. Numbers are counts over the 500-posting benchmark, held-out training postings and the live search; no individual postings or resume details are shown.
